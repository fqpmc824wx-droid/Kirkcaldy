## 1. Purpose

This policy sets out how Kirkcaldy Dental & Implant Clinic collects, uses, stores, shares and disposes of personal information about patients, prospective patients, staff and others. It covers data protection law, patient confidentiality and the security of our IT systems. It also sets out what to do if something goes wrong: a data breach, a request for information, or a cyberattack.

## 5. Policy and procedure

### 5.1 Key terms

- **Personal data** – information about a living person who can be identified from it, alone or with other information (for example name, address, email, online profile, staff HR record).
- **Special category data** – sensitive data including health information (all clinical records and medical histories) and ethnic origin. All information obtained in caring for patients is confidential.
- **Processing** – anything done with personal data: collecting, entering into Dentally, storing, viewing, sharing, deleting.
- **Data processor** – an organisation that processes data on our behalf (for example Dentally, Google, ClickSend, the IT company, payroll provider). Controllers must make sure their processors comply; both have legal liability for breaches.

### 5.2 Data protection principles

Personal information must be:

1. processed fairly, lawfully and transparently
2. collected for specified, lawful purposes and not used for anything incompatible (purpose limitation)
3. adequate, relevant and not excessive (data minimisation)
4. accurate and up to date
5. not kept longer than necessary (storage limitation)
6. kept secure – integrity and confidentiality (for example password protection, restricted access, locked cabinets)
7. and we must be able to show that we comply (accountability).

**Data protection by design and default** is a legal requirement. Whenever we set up or change a process that uses personal data (a new form, new software, a new marketing campaign), we build in data protection from the start: collect only what we need, restrict access to those who need it, and delete when no longer required. Examples: the medical history form asks only for relevant social history; only clinical staff open clinical notes; waiting-list data is deleted when no longer needed.

### 5.6 Lawful basis

1. We identify and record a lawful basis for every purpose (above) and state it in our privacy notices. For health data we also record the special category condition.
2. The PSM lists seven lawful bases: consent; contract; legal obligation; vital interests; public task; legitimate interests; and recognised legitimate interests (added by the DUAA 2025). In dentistry the bases most likely to apply are contract or legal obligation (treatment, essential employee information), legitimate interests (recalls and appointment reminders – we must justify these), and consent (marketing such as newsletters and special offers).
3. [Practice decision, confirm with DPO: NHS care – "public task"; private care – "contract"; special category condition – "health or social care" in both cases.]
4. **Consent for treatment is not the same as data protection consent.** Patient records are held under a duty of confidence and implied consent for direct care. Data protection consent is not the lawful basis for patient or staff records.
5. Where we rely on **data protection consent** (waiting list, marketing):
   - the request is prominent, concise, separate from other terms, and names the practice and any third parties relying on it (for example Meta, ClickSend)
   - people actively opt in – no pre-ticked boxes or default settings
   - there are separate (granular) options for different purposes (for example "Contact me about opening and booking" and, separately, "Send me offers and news")
   - we keep a record of who consented, when, how, and what they were told
   - withdrawing is easy (for example "Reply STOP" in every marketing SMS, and an unsubscribe link in emails)
   - children under 13 cannot give their own data protection consent; a person with parental responsibility must consent.

### 5.11 Confidentiality

1. All personal health information is confidential: notes, radiographs, photographs, treatment details, appointment records, payments, and any other personal information (for example medical conditions).
2. Every staff contract and contractor agreement contains a confidentiality clause. A breach of confidentiality is serious and can lead to disciplinary action, including dismissal.
3. Respect privacy at reception: do not discuss medical history, payments, or proof of NHS exemption where others can hear. Offer a private room. Do not read out full names and treatment details on the phone in the waiting area. Screens face away from the public.
4. Do not discuss patients outside the practice, including on social media or in personal messaging apps. Do not take photos of patients or records on personal phones.
5. Patients are told at registration, through the privacy notice, how their information is normally shared. **Specific consent is not needed** each time for normal healthcare sharing with people who "need to know", for example:
   - referral to other dental care providers or hospital services
   - discussions with the patient's doctor
   - information sent to dental laboratories.
6. Disclosure to Practitioner Services Division (PSD), NHS Fife and Dental Reference Officers is required for the NHS system to work. Dentists must provide relevant information to them **within 14 days of a request** (GDS Regulations 2010, Schedule 1, Part IV, paragraph 27).
7. If sharing is **out of the norm** and not described in the privacy notice, get the patient's specific consent (use the PSM Consent Form template) and record it.
8. If a patient asks for information to be withheld from someone, explain the consequences for their care, but the patient makes the final decision. Record the discussion.
9. Family members, carers and other third parties: only share information with the patient's consent, or where another lawful route applies (for example a welfare attorney or guardian with the relevant power, or a parent of a child who lacks capacity).

### 5.12 Disclosure to prevent serious crime or harm

1. Exceptional circumstances can override the duty of confidence: a serious public health risk, a risk of harm to others, or information required by the police for crime or taxation purposes. The DPA 2018 exemption only allows release for the stated purpose and, for crime, only if not releasing it would be likely to significantly harm police attempts to prevent crime or catch a suspect.
2. A data controller is under no obligation to disclose to a third party unless there is a statutory obligation or a court order. It may be necessary to ask the police for a court order.
3. Procedure:
   1. Ask for the request in writing, signed by someone of sufficient authority. A verbal request is acceptable only if there is an immediate risk to life or public safety; it must be followed up in writing.
   2. Check the requester's identity (for example check the warrant card and call the station back on a known number).
   3. The decision is made by the Practice Owner (or the senior dentist present), who must be able to defend it.
   4. Consider the seriousness of the crime, the risk to the public and the reason for the request.
   5. Examples where disclosure to police may be appropriate: helping identify a body (the police must have a body); an offence under the Road Traffic Act 1988; matters under the Terrorism Prevention and Investigation Measures Act 2011; a patient evading NHS charges (fraud), when directed by the authorities.
   6. Disclose only what is necessary.
   7. Routine HMRC tax enquiries: without a court order, anonymise appointment books and records before providing them.
   8. Record every disclosure in the **disclosure register** (date, requester, authority, information disclosed, reason, decision maker).
   9. If in doubt, contact the indemnity provider [name, phone] before acting.
4. Child and adult protection concerns: see 12-child-and-adult-protection.md. There is no data protection barrier to reporting suspected abuse.

### 5.14 Subject access requests (SARs)

1. Anyone can ask for a copy of their personal data. A request **need not be in writing**. Verbal requests are handled the same way: reception writes it down in the SAR log straight away and tells the Data Protection Lead.
2. **Time limit**: respond as soon as possible and within **one calendar month** of receipt.
   - If the request is unclear, ask for clarification. Under the DUAA 2025 the clock stops on the day clarification is requested and restarts when it is received.
   - The deadline can be extended by up to **two further months** if the request is complex or the person has made several requests. Tell them within the first month and explain why.
3. **Identity**: check the requester's identity before releasing anything. Third parties (for example a solicitor, or a parent) must show they are entitled to act, such as the patient's signed consent.
4. **Children**: in Scotland a child aged **12 or over** is presumed to have sufficient age and maturity to exercise their own right of access unless shown otherwise; the dentist assesses capacity and maturity. A person with parental responsibility may request the child's information if the child authorises it, if the child lacks understanding, or if it is in the child's best interests.
5. **Search**: use a reasonable and proportionate search (Dentally, imaging software, email, Google Drive, paper), focused on what was asked for (for example a treatment or date range).
6. **Fee**: free of charge, in a commonly used electronic format. A reasonable fee can be charged only for manifestly unfounded or excessive requests or for further copies.
7. **Explain** the record where needed (for example dental abbreviations and charting). State the lawful basis for processing in the response.
8. **Refusal** (rare): only if manifestly unfounded or excessive. The criteria are: [practice to define, with DPO advice]. Tell the requester within one month why, and that they can complain to the ICO and seek legal advice. Consider advice from the indemnity provider or ICO first.
9. Send the response securely and record what was sent and when.

### 5.15 Personal data breaches

A personal data breach is a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data. Examples: an email with a patient's details sent to the wrong person; a lost phone or laptop; a waiting-list spreadsheet shared publicly; Dentally accessed by someone who should not; ransomware.

Procedure:

1. **Report immediately** to the Data Protection Lead (or the Practice Owner if unavailable). Do not try to hide it. Staff will not be blamed for reporting honestly.
2. **Contain** it: recall or ask the recipient to delete an email, change passwords, remotely wipe a lost device, remove a public link.
3. **Assess** the risk to people's rights and freedoms (discrimination, damage to reputation, financial loss, loss of confidentiality, other significant disadvantage). A breach of patient confidentiality is likely to be notifiable.
4. **Report to the ICO within 72 hours** of becoming aware, if the breach is likely to result in a risk to individuals. Include the DPO's name and contact. If all the facts are not yet known, report what is known and follow up. It may help to talk to the indemnity provider first, but do not miss the 72-hour deadline.
5. **Tell the individuals** without undue delay if the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them (for example identity theft or breach of confidentiality).
6. If a processor (for example Dentally, Google, ClickSend) has a breach, they must tell us as soon as they are aware; we then report to the ICO within 72 hours.
7. **NHS breaches**: also inform NHS Fife Information Governance (fife.dataprotection@nhs.scot; switchboard 01592 643355) where NHS systems or NHS patient data are involved.
8. **Record every breach**, including those not reported to the ICO, in the breach log: what happened, data and people affected, actions, whether reported and why or why not, and lessons learned. Discuss at a practice meeting and treat it as a significant event (see 37-risk-management-and-significant-events.md).

Failure to report a notifiable breach can lead to a fine, in addition to a fine for the breach itself.

### 5.20 Cyber security

Data held locally (imaging software, intraoral scans, implant planning files, any local files) is backed up automatically [daily] to [cloud service / separate device], stored separately from the main systems and not permanently connected (so ransomware cannot reach it). Google Drive data is covered by [backup arrangement].
4. Access to backups is restricted to authorised people.
5. A test restore is done [every 6 months] and recorded.
6. The backup protocol is written down and included in the business continuity plan (38-business-continuity-plan.md).

### 5.21 Procedure if a cyberattack is suspected

1. **Do not pay any ransom.**
2. Disconnect the affected device from the network (unplug the cable / turn off Wi-Fi). Do not switch it off unless told to by IT support.
3. **Within 30 minutes of discovery (ideally):**
   - Contact **Practitioner Services Division (PSD)** on 0345 034 2458 (nss.psddental@nhs.scot) to report it and have the CAT20/SWAN connection cut to protect the NHS network. If NSS cannot disable it remotely straight away, unplug the SWAN router.
   - Phone the **NHS Fife Information Governance Lead** (fife.dataprotection@nhs.scot; switchboard 01592 643355) immediately. (If the owner has practices in other Board areas, tell them too.)
   - Phone the **IT support company** [phone] to report the attack and arrange cleansing of the systems.
4. Contact **Dentally** support [phone/email] (and other software suppliers) to report the attack and discuss recovering data once the system is clean.
5. Report to **Police Scotland** (101) if applicable.
6. Report to the **ICO within 72 hours** of discovering a personal data breach, if applicable (see 5.15).
7. Change passwords; check Google Workspace and Meta account activity for unknown logins.
8. Follow the business continuity plan to keep seeing patients where safe.
9. Record it as a data breach and significant event and review lessons learned.

Keep a printed copy of these contacts at reception and off-site.
