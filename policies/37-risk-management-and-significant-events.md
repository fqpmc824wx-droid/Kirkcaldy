## 1. Purpose

Risk management means spotting where things might go wrong and putting systems in place to reduce the chance of harm, an adverse incident or a poor outcome. It is one of the seven pillars of clinical governance. A key part of it is learning from mistakes and near misses.

This policy sets out how Kirkcaldy Dental & Implant Clinic:

- identifies, assesses and controls clinical and non-clinical risks;
- keeps a practice **risk register**;
- reports and records incidents, however minor, in a no-blame way;
- carries out **Significant Event Analysis (SEA)** and shares the learning;
- links incidents to other duties (complaints, accidents, Duty of Candour and statutory reporting).

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to all staff, including the Practice Owner, associates, locums, trainees and contractors working on site. It covers all clinical, administrative, systems, personnel, financial and environmental risks in the practice, NHS and private.

Raising concerns and whistleblowing are covered separately in 07-raising-concerns-whistleblowing.md. Business continuity for major incidents is in 38-business-continuity-plan.md. Health and safety risk assessments (fire, COSHH, legionella, general premises) are in their own policies but feed into the risk register.

## 5. Policy and procedure

### 5.1 What a risk is

A risk is the possibility of an adverse event that would expose the dental team, patients or visitors to harm. Risks fall into two broad, overlapping groups:

- **Clinical risk** – a patient's or staff member's well-being is compromised by the provision of dental care. Examples: ineffective infection prevention and control, inappropriate PPE, radiation protection, COSHH (including mercury), legionella, latex and other allergies.
- **Non-clinical risk** – administrative errors, premises or surroundings. Examples: patient communication (consent, child and adult protection), record-keeping (including medical histories), work equipment failure, staff recruitment and employment (CPD, registration, indemnity, immunisation, PVG), personal well-being, health, stress and fitness to practise, financial management, health and safety (slips, fire), premises, team communication and quality improvement.

### 5.2 The risk management system

The practice's system has these parts:

1. **Written policies and procedures** for all aspects of practice activity (this policy set, numbered 01–40), which staff read, understand and sign.
2. **Risk assessment** of identified risks, recorded on the PSM *Risk Assessment* template and summarised in the risk register.
3. **Incident reporting and SEA** (5.6 and 5.7).
4. **Complaints procedure** and good record-keeping.
5. **Staff training** and good communication with patients and the team.
6. **Quality assurance** – quality improvement activity and audit to check that staff do what the policies say (see 39-quality-improvement-and-audit.md).
7. **Regular review** of policies and the risk register, with staff told of changes.

Risks are discussed at every team meeting, and all staff are encouraged to raise risks they have noticed.

### 5.3 How to carry out a risk assessment

Use the PSM *Risk Assessment (Blank)* template (see the *Worked Example*):

1. **Identify the risk area** (e.g. administrative, clinical, financial).
2. **Decide who might be harmed and how** – patients, staff, visitors, contractors; children, vulnerable adults, pregnant staff.
3. **Evaluate the risk and decide on precautions (controls).** Score likelihood (1–5) × severity (1–5) to give a rating [practice decision – scoring matrix: 1–4 low, 5–9 medium, 10–16 high, 20–25 very high]. List existing controls and further action needed.
4. **Record the findings and take action** – name the person responsible and a target date.
5. **Review and update** the assessment – at the set review date, after an incident, or when something changes.
6. **Share the findings** and any changes with all staff.

5. Before opening, the Practice Manager completes an initial register covering every area in 5.4.

### 5.6 Incident reporting

An incident is anything that went wrong, or nearly went wrong. What counts as an incident is subjective – what seems minor to one person may seem major to another – so **report everything, however minor**. Examples: lab work not delivered for an appointment; an entry made in the wrong patient record; a near miss with a sharp; a patient fainting; a missed referral; an X-ray taken of the wrong patient; a lost USB stick; a failed steriliser cycle; a positive event worth learning from.

Events that are not reported are likely to happen again.

**How to report**

1. Make the situation safe and look after anyone affected first.
2. Tell the person in charge of the session [and the relevant policy lead] straight away.
3. Complete the **Incident Reporting Form** (PSM template) [paper copies in [location] / electronic form at [location]] by the end of the day: what happened, when, where, who was involved, immediate action, and any harm.
4. The Practice Manager logs it in the incident log with a reference number.

Information about incidents can also come from patient complaints, the accident book and verbal reports from team members – the Practice Manager checks these sources [monthly] for incidents not yet reported.

**No blame**

The system is non-threatening and has no element of blame. Staff report without fear of blame or punishment. The aim is to learn and to stop it happening again. Staff are told about the system at induction and the Practice Owner demonstrates confidence in it by acting on reports. (Concerns about wrongdoing or patient safety that staff do not feel able to raise in this way can be raised under 07-raising-concerns-whistleblowing.md.)

### 5.7 Significant Event Analysis (SEA)

SEA looks back at an event – positive or negative – to establish what happened, why, and what can be learned, so that changes are made to improve patient care and practice systems.

**When to carry out an SEA**

- after **every** medical emergency;
- after any incident that caused, or could have caused, harm;
- after any reportable incident (radiation, Duty of Candour, data breach, CD incident);
- for recurring minor incidents of the same type;
- for a positive event where the team wants to understand what went well;
- whenever the Practice Owner or a policy lead decides it would be useful.

**How (following NES eSEA guidance on TURAS)**

1. **Gather information** – the incident form, records, statements from those involved, timeline.
2. **Meet** – discuss the event fully with all members of the team, ideally at a practice meeting, where everyone can contribute and suggest solutions. Keep the discussion factual and blame-free.
3. **Analyse** – what happened; why it happened (contributing factors: people, communication, equipment, systems, environment); what went well; what could have been done differently.
4. **Agree learning and actions** – risk-reduction measures, with an owner and date for each. Update the risk register and policies if needed.
5. **Write it up** – on the [NES eSEA report format / practice SEA form], without patient-identifiable information where possible.
6. **Share** – feed back to all staff; follow up actions at the next meeting.
7. **Review** – check the actions worked.

A well-documented SEA can count as QI activity (enhanced SEA) – see 39-quality-improvement-and-audit.md.
