## 1. Purpose

This policy protects staff and patients from allergy to natural rubber latex (NRL) and from work-related contact dermatitis. It covers:
- prevention of allergies among the dental team;
- protection for sensitised staff;
- management of patients with allergies;
- prevention and management of work-related skin problems.

## 2. Scope

All staff, self-employed clinicians, trainees and cleaners, and all patients and visitors. It covers gloves and every other product that may contain NRL or skin irritants (rubber dam, prophy cups, orthodontic elastics, BP cuffs, bite blocks, soaps, hand rubs, disinfectants).

**Background.** There are two types of NRL allergy:
- **Type I – immediate hypersensitivity**, caused by natural latex proteins. This can be life-threatening (anaphylaxis). Contact urticaria may also occur in sensitised people.
- **Type IV – delayed hypersensitivity**, caused by chemical additives used to make NRL products.

**Irritant contact dermatitis** is not a true allergy, but it is the most common skin problem in dental teams. Its main causes are chemicals in both latex and nitrile gloves, soaps and hand cleansers, and "wet work" (frequent hand washing or surface cleaning). Decontamination chemicals and X-ray processing chemicals can also cause it. Powdered latex gloves increase sensitisation because the powder carries latex proteins into the air.

## 3. Legislation and guidance

As cited in the PSM:
- Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002 – the employer must assess the risk NRL poses to staff and patients
- Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 – occupational dermatitis confirmed by a doctor is reportable
- National Infection Prevention and Control Manual (NIPCM), NHS National Services Scotland – glove selection, hand hygiene (Ch 1.2) and PPE (Ch 1.4); powdered latex gloves should not be used
- HSE: *Latex allergies in health and social care*; skin care posters; *How do I prevent skin problems in my business?*
- Health Protection Scotland: *Latex allergy: occupational aspects of management* (2008)
- Anaphylaxis Campaign (incorporating Latex Allergy Support Group)
- UK Medicines Information: *Which local dental anaesthetics are latex-free?*

## 5. Policy / procedure

### 5.1 COSHH assessment and product choice

1. The Health & Safety Lead completes a COSHH assessment for NRL (PSM COSHH Risk Assessment template), plus assessments for hand hygiene and cleaning products (18-coshh-policy.md).
2. **Gloves:** the practice uses powder-free gloves only – powder-free low-protein latex or nitrile – selected in line with the NIPCM. Powdered latex gloves are never bought or used. [Practice decision: the practice stocks nitrile gloves as standard and is latex-glove-free.]
3. A list of products that may contain NRL, with latex-free alternatives, is kept with the COSHH folder: [list – e.g. rubber dam (non-latex available), prophy cups, orthodontic elastics, BP cuff, tourniquet, bite blocks, mixing bowls].
4. Note: since January 2018, NRL is not used in the manufacture, containment or packaging of any UK local anaesthetic products, so cartridge seals are no longer a latex risk.

### 5.2 Prevention among the dental team

1. Staff are made aware of the risks of NRL at induction.
2. Staff protect their skin barrier, especially those with a history of asthma, hay fever or flexural eczema, or who often get skin irritation, as these increase the risk of latex allergy.
3. Use suitable hand care products at the end of each session, especially if skin tends to be dry.
4. Follow the hand hygiene policy (25-hand-hygiene-policy.md): use the gentle soaps provided, rinse well, and dry thoroughly with good-quality soft paper towels. Do not wear gloves longer than needed, and put them on only when hands are fully dry.
5. Emollients are provided in dispensers that prevent cross-contamination [locations].

### 5.3 Preventing work-related contact dermatitis

The practice reduces skin exposure to allergens and irritants by:
1. Using automation where possible (e.g. washer-disinfector for instruments, reducing manual cleaning).
2. Introducing less hazardous products when available (e.g. digital radiography instead of processing chemicals).
3. Providing suitable PPE, bearing in mind that gloves can themselves cause dermatitis.
4. Providing effective hand hygiene products that minimise skin damage.
5. Training staff in using equipment and gloves, correct hand cleaning and skin care (e.g. regular moisturiser).
6. Providing good hand-drying facilities.
7. Running a **skin-check system**: staff check their own hands [weekly] using the HSE skin check guide, and the Health & Safety Lead asks about skin problems at [monthly / quarterly] one-to-ones and records it. New staff have a baseline check at induction.

### 5.4 If a staff member develops a skin problem or suspected allergy

1. The staff member tells the Health & Safety Lead straight away. Signs include redness, itching, dryness, cracking, blistering, hives, or symptoms such as runny nose, sore eyes or wheeze when using gloves.
2. The Health & Safety Lead arranges referral through the staff member's GP or the practice's occupational health service [provider] – to a dermatologist or immunologist if an NRL allergy is suspected.
3. Meanwhile the staff member is given alternative non-latex gloves and the work environment is adapted.
4. If a doctor confirms occupational dermatitis, it is **reportable under RIDDOR** as an occupational disease (17-health-and-safety-policy.md, section 5.11).
5. The COSHH assessment and this policy are reviewed.

### 5.5 Protection for sensitised staff

1. If a staff member is sensitive to NRL, adapt the work environment to avoid unnecessary exposure that could lead to more severe reactions.
2. **Type I allergy diagnosed:** the person may still be able to work in a clinical setting depending on severity – take advice from occupational health. Advise them to avoid NRL proteins at home and at work. Assess whether to replace latex gloves and whether the whole environment should be NRL-free (no colleagues using latex gloves). Encourage them to wear a medic alert bracelet.
3. **Type IV allergy diagnosed:** provide non-sterile nitrile gloves that do not contain the specific chemical. Ask a dermatologist for advice on choosing them.
4. Individual arrangements are recorded in a confidential individual risk assessment and reviewed [annually / on change].

### 5.6 Management of patients with allergies

1. Every patient completes a medical history at registration and it is updated at every course of treatment. It includes the PSM Latex Questionnaire questions, so we can establish a true history and decide whether a latex-free environment is needed.
2. A latex allergy is recorded as a **medical alert in Dentally** and is visible at check-in and in the surgery.
3. Each case is risk assessed by the dentist.
4. For a patient who needs a latex-free environment:
   - book the first appointment of the session where possible [practice decision];
   - remove all NRL items from the surgery and use latex-free alternatives;
   - all staff in the room wear non-latex gloves;
   - make sure the anaphylaxis kit (adrenaline) is ready (34-medical-emergencies-policy.md).
5. A latex poster (PSM Latex Poster) is displayed in the waiting room asking patients to tell us about allergies.
6. If a patient has a reaction, manage it as a medical emergency (anaphylaxis protocol), record it, and investigate (37-risk-management-and-significant-events.md).

## 6. Records and monitoring

- NRL COSHH assessment and product list: COSHH folder; reviewed annually and when products change.
- Staff skin checks, referrals and individual risk assessments: confidential staff health records, kept by the Health & Safety Lead.
- Patient latex questionnaires and alerts: Dentally.
- Reactions and RIDDOR reports: accident book and SEA file.
- Annual check [practice decision]: audit that only powder-free gloves are stocked and that latex alerts are present for patients who declare an allergy.

## 7. Training

- Induction: NRL risks, types of allergy, glove choice, hand care, skin checks, how to report symptoms.
- Hand hygiene and skin care training (with 25-hand-hygiene-policy.md), refreshed [annually].
- Clinical team: preparing a latex-free surgery; recognising and managing anaphylaxis (annual medical emergencies training).
